A new early-stage mouse study from the Israel Institute of Biological Research examines the brain immune response in neuronopathic Gaucher disease. According to the authors, the Gaucher model mice showed greater resistance to infection with Sindbis virus in the brain, and they link this protection to the pre-existing type I interferon response. The team compared mice that modeled the full neuronopathic disease with control mice, and exposed both groups to the virus.

Gaucher’s disease is a common lysosomal storage disorder named after French physician Philippe Gaucher, who described it in 1882. [1] Lysosomes are sacs of enzymes within cells that digest large molecules and pass fragments to other parts of the cell for recycling. [3] When the lysosome doesn’t function normally, excess products destined for breakdown and recycling are stored in the cell. [3] Lysosomal storage diseases (LSDs) are a group of over 70 rare inherited metabolic disorders. [3] The biochemical basis of the disease was elucidated in the 1960’s by Roscoe Brady. [1] A hereditary deficiency of the enzyme glucocerebrosidase (also called glucosylceramidase), which acts on glucocerebroside, causes Gaucher’s disease. [1] Glucocerebroside is a sphingolipid, which are known to participate in inflammation and apoptosis. [1] When the enzyme doesn’t work properly, the lipid builds up inside cells, particularly in white blood cells and especially in macrophages (mononuclear leukocytes). Macrophages that clear these cells cannot eliminate the waste product, which accumulates in fibrils and turns the macrophages into ‘Gaucher cells.’ The early signs of Gaucher’s disease are anemia, low blood platelet count, swelling of the liver and spleen, and a low white blood cell count. [1]

Gaucher’s disease has three common clinical subtypes. [1] Type I (non-neuropathic) is the most common and least severe. [1] It has some residual enzyme activity, accounting for the lack of neuropathology in this type. [1] About 80 known GBA gene mutations are grouped into three main types. [1] Type II (acute infantile neuropathic) typically begins within 6 months of birth. [1] Symptoms include enlarged liver and spleen, extensive progressive brain damage, eye movement disorders, spasticity, seizures, limb rigidity, and poor ability to suck and swallow. [1] Type II symptoms are progressive and the children usually die by age two. [1] People with type III (chronic neuropathic) can begin to show symptoms at any time in childhood or in adulthood. [1] This type progresses more slowly than the others. It has slowly progressive neurologic symptoms that are milder than type II. [1] Neuronal damage associated with the disease was discovered in the 1920’s. [1] The exact mechanism of neurotoxicity is not understood, but is thought to involve a reaction to glucosylsphingosine. [1]

A person has to inherit two copies of the defective gene to have type I, II, or III Gaucher’s disease. [1] If they only have one copy, they’re considered a carrier. Carrier rates of GBA variants are also notably elevated in certain populations, including Ashkenazi Jews. For example, the sickle cell trait offering some protection against malaria is the best-known example of such an advantage. Whether GBA carriers have protection against viral infections is an open question. Gaucher’s disease is treated with enzyme replacement therapy with recombinant glucocerebrosidase. [1] Enzyme replacement with intravenous recombinant glucocerebrosidase can decrease the size of the liver and spleen, reduce skeletal abnormalities, and reverse other manifestations for type I and most type III. [1]

The role of inflammatory processes in Gaucher’s disease is poorly elucidated. [1] Markers of macrophage activation are elevated in people with Gaucher’s disease. [1] Inflammatory processes may contribute to the pathogenesis of the disease. β-Glucocerebrosidase is also called acid β-glucosidase, D-glucosyl-N-acylsphingosine glucohydrolase, or GCase. [2] It’s localized in the lysosome and remains associated with the lysosomal membrane. [2] β-Glucocerebrosidase is a protein of 497 amino acids, which are encoded by the GBA gene on chromosome 1. [2]

Sindbis virus is a mosquito-borne alphavirus widely used in laboratory research as a model of viral infection of the central nervous system. They found that the brains of the affected animals mounted a sustained innate immune response. This was characterized by the production of type I interferon, the main antiviral cytokine, in the brain. The finding comes from animals, not people, so it’s preliminary. It affects both males and females. [1] Type I interferons are cytokines, proteins that can enhance the immune response. They switch on hundreds of interferon-stimulated genes, which put nearby cells into an “antiviral state,” making them less hospitable to viral replication and better at alerting the rest of the immune system. In mice, Sindbis virus can infect neurons and cause encephalitis. The higher level of type I interferons found in the brains of neuronopathic Gaucher disease mice may explain this protection.

Eliglustat (Cerdelga) was approved in 2014. [1] Ambroxol has recently been shown to increase the activity of the lysosomal enzyme glucocerebrosidase. [3]
