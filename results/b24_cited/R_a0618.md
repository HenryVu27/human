| In The Future of Marriage in Western Civilization, 1936, Westermarck rejected his earlier definition and provisionally defined marriage as "a relation of one or more men to one or more women that is recognized by custom or law". [1]

| In 1951, the anthropologist Kathleen Gough described marriage as "a relationship established between a woman and one or more other persons, which provides that a child born to the woman under circumstances not prohibited by rules concerning the relationship will be accorded full birth-status rights common to normal members of his society or social stratum". [1]

| The history of marriage includes many arranged marriages and polygamy, in which a man is allowed to have more than one wife. [1] In this form, a woman lost ownership of her property and came under her husband's authority. [2]

| During the reign of Augustus, a father had to give a valid reason for not consenting to a marriage between his child and the person his child saw fit to marry. [2]

| In Roman law marriage was voluntary, and marriage contracts were negotiated between the families of the prospective spouses.

From the earliest days of the Christian faith, Christians have honored holy matrimony as a divinely blessed, lifelong, monogamous union between a man and a woman. [3]

| The age of lawful consent to marriage was 12 for girls and 14 for boys. [2]

| Roman law recognized three kinds of marriage: confarreatio, coemptio, and usus. [2] The first form, confarreatio, was identical to traditional Roman marriage, however it was only performed by the highest social classes, due to the complex ritual and religious ceremony involved. [2]

| According to Livy, Romulus and his men abducted the Sabine maidens but promised them an honorable marriage with the benefits of property, citizenship, and children. [2]

| In classical Roman law, marriage required no specific ceremony. However, Roman marriages usually involved several formalities.

| Marriage, as a religious institution, can differ from marriage as a civil institution. [3] Where marriage is purely a civil institution, marriage can be created by obtaining a marriage license to fill in the legal formalities. [3] However, where marriage is also a religious institution, being married in the eyes of a religion is also a prerequisite for a recognized marriage, and pre-marital requirements of the religion must also be completed before a religious marriage can occur.

| In the history of Western societies, marriage once comprised a voluntary union between a man and a woman and was intended by the parties to be a permanent union. The history of marriage has undergone a number of changes, including the acceptance of marriages between partners of different races, or of different religions, and an increase in the number of married couples who have chosen not to have children.

The Christian Church began developing its own views on marriage, marrying couples within the church and under church rules. After the Protestants reformed marriage, marriage came to be recognized as more than just a civil contract, but also as having a spiritual dimension. In Protestants, the term "holy matrimony" was used. [3]
