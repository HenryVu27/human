The one who commits the tortious act is called a tortfeasor. [1]

Most modern tort cases are settled through claims adjustment and defended by insurance lawyers. [1]

The two most common forms of tortious injury are intentional torts and negligent torts. Intentional torts occur when someone intentionally affects someone else's well-being or property. Negligent torts occur when someone fails to exercise the degree of care that society expects to protect other people or their property.

Negligence is a tort because it involves the breach of a duty to exercise reasonable care, and the standard for determining liability is based on whether a reasonable person would have exercised such care. A negligence claim is not actionable unless the plaintiff can demonstrate that the defendant had a duty of care to the plaintiff under the circumstances, that the defendant breached that duty, and that the damages were the proximate cause of the breach.

Common law courts began shifting toward a no-liability regime for products, except for fraud or breach of express warranty, by developing the doctrine of caveat emptor (buyer beware) in the early 1600's. [2] However, strict liability for products was not adopted in the United States until the twentieth century.

The Restatement codified the Greenman doctrine in Section 402(A) (1964). [2]

As of 2018, the five states that have rejected strict liability are Delaware, Massachusetts, Michigan, North Carolina, and Virginia. [2]

Tort claims typically seek compensation for injury to a person, property, reputation, or economic interest. The plaintiff must demonstrate that the actions or lack of action was the legally recognizable cause of the harm, that is, the plaintiff must prove that the defendant's actions or lack of action were the cause of the harm. [1] Examples of tort claims include battery, negligence, and defamation.

Negligent torts have four elements: duty, breach of duty, cause, and damages. A person has a duty to act as a reasonable person would act under similar circumstances. A person breaches their duty when they fail to act in the way that a reasonable person would act under similar circumstances. The breaching person must have been the cause of the harm, which is the legal injury. Damages are the harm suffered by the injured party, which may include monetary loss, pain and suffering, and loss of property or reputation.

A person who suffers the harm may sue the tortfeasor for damages. [1] The tortfeasor may have an affirmative defense, such as assumption of risk, that reduces or eliminates the liability for damages.

Tort liability is not necessarily the same as liability under contract law. As such, tort law generally comprises a separate set of causes of action.

Strict liability is a legal standard that holds a person liable for the consequences of his or her action without a finding of fault, negligence, or intent. [3] It is sometimes contrasted with tort law, where intentional wrongdoing may lead to punitive damages.

The liability insurance industry has had an important role in developing tort law.

The claimant need only prove that the tort occurred and that the defendant was responsible. [3] The defendant can then raise a defense of absence of fault, which may eliminate or reduce liability. [3]

In 1842, the English case of Winterbottom v Wright exemplifies the privity of contract defense. [2]

In the United States, Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act generally shields online platforms from liability for content posted by their users, but the person who wrote a defamatory online statement can still be held responsible, but the hosting platform usually cannot. In the United States, product liability law developed primarily through state court case law and the Restatements of the Law produced by the American Law Institute (ALI). [2]
