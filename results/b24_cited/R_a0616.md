A stock market bubble (sometimes referred to simply as an asset bubble or a speculative bubble) is the rapid escalation of asset prices based on unjustified exuberance. [2] Bubbles can last for years until the price suddenly plummets. The market crash of 1929 is the classic example of a bubble and burst, but there have been many others since.

The Mississippi Scheme in France and the South Sea bubble in England were two famous early stock market bubbles. [2]

The Roaring Twenties stock market bubble (which caused the Great Depression) and the United States housing bubble (which caused the Great Recession) are examples of debt bubbles. [1] These bubbles usually start with a legitimate boom followed by overzealous speculation, and they end with a crash. The Japanese Nikkei index peaked at the end of 1989, and has not returned to that level for over 30 years.

The dot-com bubble of the late 1990’s is the textbook stock market bubble. Between 1995 and its March 2000 peak, the Nasdaq Composite rose 400%. [3] The Nasdaq peaked in March 2000, and lost roughly three-quarters of its value over the next couple of years.

A bubble forms when the price of an asset rises far beyond what its underlying value can justify.

The “mechanism” that is used to explain the development of bubbles is a positive feedback loop. [2] When the price of an asset rises, people notice and are inclined to buy some of that asset themselves, in the hope that the prices will continue to rise and they can make a profit. [2] This demand drives prices up further, which leads to more people buying. [2]

This type of thinking helps to propagate the bubble, as everyone invests with the intent of finding a greater fool. [2]

Most dot-com companies incurred net operating losses from heavy spending on advertising and promotions to build market share or mind share quickly through network effects. [3]

By contrast, the S&P 500 fell more than 50% from its 2007 peak to its March 2009 low. It took until 2015 for the Nasdaq to regain its 2000 peak.

Debt bubbles are quite different. A debt bubble is characterized by intangible or credit-based investments with little ability to satisfy a growing demand in a non-existent market. [1]

The 2008 financial crisis is the defining credit bubble example; bank stocks collapsed and credit froze. Rising oil prices in 2008 created a commodity bubble, hitting producers first. Energy and mining stocks can soar in the run-up and plunge when prices reverse.

In 2008, the housing boom turned to bust, and stock prices plunged. The Nikkei peaked at a price-earnings ratio of 80 during the Japanese asset price bubble of 1991. [3] Housing booms in the mid-2000’s were classic market bubbles; home prices rose year after year, and many buyers assumed that they would always.

Behavioral finance theory attributes stock market bubbles to cognitive biases that lead to groupthink and herd behavior. [2]

Bubbles or bull markets are part of investing and cannot always be avoided, but they can be detected with these warning signs:

Everyone’s talking about an investment: taxi drivers, coworkers, and your social media feeds are all pushing it. This might indicate that the enthusiasm is peaking and caution is called for.

Prices are rising or falling at a rate that is difficult to justify, based on the underlying value of the stock.

Companies have weak or non-existent profits, and they’re valued on promises rather than results.

Try to stay away from these types of investments.

A good strategy to avoid bubbles is to simply do your own research instead of following the hype. Don’t let the fear of missing out drive you to make an investment decision.

If the main reason to buy is that prices are rising, pause and reconsider.
