Gross domestic product (GDP) is the most widely cited measure of a country's economic performance and it is often referred to as the world's most powerful statistical indicator of national development and progress. National income comparisons are frequently made using purchasing power parity (PPP) to adjust for cost of living differences. [2] In 2017 the Central Bank of Ireland created "modified GNI" (GNI*) as a more appropriate statistic. [2] This statistic removed some of the material distortion caused by the tax planning activities of foreign multinationals in Ireland. [2] GDP per capita has been used as a measure of standard of living, however it is only a loose approximation. [2]

GDP per capita is not a measure of personal income, which makes its use as a living standard indicator problematic. [2]

The underground economy, or black market, consists of legal goods and services exchanged off the books to avoid taxes or regulation (for example cash-paid babysitting, unreported tips, or unreported tips).

Critics argue that GDP is a poor measure of economic well-being, for instance it does not take into account the value of unpaid work, e.g. [1] Valuable natural resources are treated as free goods, as if they would last forever.

Natural resources

The economic value of natural resources is not necessarily reflected in a country's GDP. GDP also ignores the detrimental effects associated with the exploring, sourcing, and harvesting of natural resources. When measuring GDP, the damages from environmental degradation are a portion of the external cost not accounted for in the GDP.

The environmental quality of the land and water is not fully accounted for in the GDP calculations.

GDP becomes more inaccurate when it is used to compare economies across countries because purchasing power varies so widely. In 2015, for example, Irish GDP was 143% of Irish GNI*. [2]

The value of natural capital is not measured at all.

The OECD Better Life Index provides an alternative to gross domestic product that reflects quality of life. [1]

GDP is not necessarily a measure of a person's (or group of people's) standard of living. [2] It is simply the monetary value of the total goods and services produced by a country. A country may have a high GDP, but low standard of living, if most of its production is owned by a small number of people.

GDP accounting uses the concept of national income accounting which was established by Simon Kuznets in 1934. [1] Although GDP had been estimated since the 1934, it was in the 1944 that GDP became widely used, due to the economic upheavals of World War II. [1]

Standards of living

The standards of living between different countries can be compared using GDP figures. The GDP per capita is especially useful because it is not affected by the size of a country's population. If a country's GDP increases, it is likely that the GDP per capita will also increase. However, if a country's population is increasing as well, then the higher GDP figure may not reflect an increase in standard of living for each person in that country.

GDP per capita does not take into account the cost of living and inflation rates. [1] The GDP per capita can be adjusted to account for inflation and cost of living.

A more useful measure of a standard of living is to combine GDP per capita with GDP per capita of purchasing power parity. [1] Purchasing power parity takes the cost of living into account. [2] Purchasing power parity is a better measure of standard of living because it compares how much people from different countries can actually buy with the money they earn, instead of simply comparing how much money they earn.

If we just used nominal GDP per capita to compare standards of living, then countries with high costs of living would appear to have a higher standard of living than countries with low costs of living. PPP largely removes the exchange rate problem, but leaves other problems, and does not accurately reflect the value of economic output in international trade. [2]

If economic growth results in damage to the environment, GDP would continue to increase but the standard of living would actually decrease.

The OECD Better Life Index uses a number of other indicators that are not included in GDP to measure the standard of living in a country. [1]
